Sirach

The teaching of a Jerusalem sage who joined Israel’s wisdom tradition to Torah, worship, friendship, family life, social responsibility, and the memory of Israel’s ancestors.

Early second century BCE Originally composed in Hebrew Translated into Greek by the author’s grandson Canonical in Catholic and Orthodox traditions
This author is not anonymous.

Unlike 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach is associated with a historical Jerusalem sage: Ben Sira. His grandson’s Greek prologue also gives us unusually valuable information about the book’s translation and transmission.

How NLDG is approaching this writing

This page is for historical, biblical, and theological study. Sirach is Scripture in Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox traditions and is not part of the Protestant Old Testament canon. Its Jewish origin, Christian reception, rediscovered Hebrew manuscripts, and challenging social assumptions all deserve to be named rather than flattened.

What is Sirach?

Sirach is a large collection of Jewish wisdom teaching composed in Jerusalem by a sage commonly called Ben Sira. The Hebrew work was completed around the early second century BCE, commonly by about 175 BCE.

The book addresses ordinary and religious life together: friendship, speech, wealth and poverty, family, discipline, worship, prayer, leadership, generosity, honor and shame, suffering, creation, death, Torah, and the fear of God.

The Greek title tradition gives us the name Sirach, while the Latin church often called the book Ecclesiasticus. That Latin title should not be confused with Ecclesiastes, which is a different biblical wisdom book.

Text & transmission

From Hebrew Jerusalem to Greek-speaking readers

Hebrew composition

Ben Sira wrote in Hebrew in Jerusalem during the early second century BCE. His work belongs directly to the continuing Hebrew wisdom tradition rather than originating as a Greek composition.

The grandson’s prologue

The Greek translator identifies himself as the author’s grandson and explains that he came to Egypt and translated the work for readers who wanted to live according to the Law. This prologue is an unusually valuable ancient witness to authorship and translation.

Greek transmission

The Greek translation became the form through which Sirach circulated widely in Christian communities and entered Greek Christian biblical tradition.

Hebrew rediscovery

Beginning in the late nineteenth century, substantial medieval Hebrew manuscripts were recovered from the Cairo Geniza. Additional ancient Hebrew witnesses were found at Masada and among the Dead Sea Scrolls.

More than one textual form

The surviving Hebrew, Greek, Latin, and Syriac traditions do not always line up word for word. Modern critical editions therefore compare multiple witnesses rather than pretending one surviving manuscript preserves every stage of the book identically.

Why this matters: The rediscovered Hebrew material confirmed that Sirach was genuinely a Hebrew Jewish work, even though Christians had known it for centuries mainly through translations.

Structure

A wisdom teacher, then a gallery of Israel’s ancestors

Sirach is more discursive than a tightly outlined narrative, but two large movements are easy to recognize.

Sirach 1–43

Wisdom for worship and ordinary life

Ben Sira teaches about the fear of God, speech, discipline, friendship, family, wealth, poverty, generosity, leadership, worship, prayer, creation, sickness, death, and many other parts of daily life.

Short sayings sit beside longer poems and instructions, much like the varied forms found in Proverbs.
Sirach 44–50

Praise of the Ancestors

The sage retells Israel’s history through a sequence of remembered figures including Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Aaron, Joshua, Samuel, David, Solomon, Elijah, Hezekiah, Josiah, and others.

This is one of the earliest extended wisdom reflections on Israel’s sacred history.
Sirach 51

Thanksgiving and final invitation

The closing material includes thanksgiving to God and a final invitation to seek Wisdom through disciplined learning.

Textual witnesses differ in some details of the closing chapter, another reason modern editions compare multiple ancient forms.

Wisdom and Torah

Wisdom takes root in Israel’s covenant life

Sirach continues the personified-Wisdom tradition of Proverbs, but it makes an important move: divine Wisdom is closely connected with Israel’s Torah. Sirach 24 celebrates Wisdom’s cosmic origin and then portrays her as taking up residence among God’s people.

This means wisdom is not merely clever observation about how life works. To Ben Sira, genuine wisdom involves fear of the Lord, obedience, worship, instruction, and life within Israel’s covenant tradition.

That synthesis is historically important. It shows a stage of Jewish thought in which wisdom and Torah are increasingly interpreted together rather than as separate streams.

Social ethics

Friendship, money, speech, worship, and justice

Friendship

Sirach 6 offers one of antiquity’s memorable treatments of loyal friendship, warning against shallow relationships while praising a faithful friend as something precious.

Speech

Ben Sira repeatedly connects wisdom with restraint in speech, careful listening, truthful words, and awareness that careless talk can destroy trust and honor.

Wealth and generosity

The book recognizes economic realities while warning against arrogance, exploitation, and misplaced trust in wealth. Generosity toward the poor belongs to faithful life.

Worship and justice

Sirach refuses to let ritual substitute for righteousness. Sirach 34 sharply criticizes offerings built on exploitation and presents injustice against the poor as incompatible with acceptable worship.

Testing and endurance

Sirach 2 prepares those who serve God for testing rather than promising an easy life, connecting perseverance with trust in God.

Honor and community

Many instructions arise from an ancient honor-shame culture. Those social assumptions help explain the book, but they should not automatically become timeless social rules.

A necessary reading caution

Sirach’s portrayal of women requires context and discernment

Sirach contains both positive images of wives, mothers, Wisdom personified as female, and family faithfulness, and passages that speak about women through strongly patriarchal assumptions. Some sayings make sweeping negative generalizations that modern scholars have rightly criticized.

NLDG will not hide those texts, excuse harmful generalizations, or turn ancient household assumptions into permission for contempt, control, or mistreatment of women. They should be studied within the social world of the author, compared with the wider witness of Scripture, and evaluated in light of Jesus’ treatment of women and the equal dignity of people made in God’s image.

Guardrail: Historical context explains why a text sounds the way it does; context does not require Christians to reproduce every ancient social assumption as a timeless command.

Canon and reception

Who receives Sirach as Scripture?

Roman Catholic tradition

Sirach is part of the Catholic Old Testament and is received as inspired, canonical Scripture. The Catholic title tradition also commonly uses Ecclesiasticus.

Eastern Orthodox traditions

Orthodox Christianity receives Sirach within the longer Old Testament canon and uses its teaching in the life and worship of the church.

Protestant traditions

Sirach is not part of the Protestant Old Testament canon. Historically, many Protestant Bibles printed it among the Apocrypha for reading and instruction without giving it the same canonical authority.

Jewish reception

Sirach is not part of the Jewish biblical canon, but its Jewish reception history is unusually substantial. Hebrew manuscripts, rabbinic echoes, and medieval Jewish preservation show that the book continued to be read and valued in Jewish communities.

Important: “Not in the Jewish canon” does not mean “unknown to Jews.” Sirach is one of the strongest examples of a Jewish work that remained influential even outside the final Jewish biblical collection.

Biblical connections

Where Sirach intersects with canonical Scripture

Recent scholarship distinguishes close parallels from formal quotation. The New Testament does not explicitly quote Sirach as Scripture, but several sayings sit strikingly near later New Testament teaching.

Close ethical parallel

Sirach 28:2 and Matthew 6:12–15

Sirach connects forgiving another person’s wrongdoing with seeking forgiveness from God. Jesus’ teaching in the Lord’s Prayer and its explanation makes a closely related connection.

Themes to notice

What Sirach contributes to the biblical world

Wisdom and Torah together

Sirach shows Jewish wisdom becoming increasingly explicit about its home within covenant, law, worship, and the story of Israel.

Formation through discipline

Wisdom requires patience, correction, listening, practice, and humility rather than merely collecting information.

Friendship as moral formation

Friends can expose character, strengthen fidelity, and become part of a wise life before God.

Justice and worship belong together

The treatment of the poor is not separate from religion; unjust wealth can corrupt the very worship offered to God.

Remembering Israel’s ancestors

History becomes moral instruction as past leaders, priests, prophets, kings, and faithful ancestors are remembered for present formation.

Jewish wisdom before the New Testament

Sirach helps bridge earlier biblical wisdom and the Jewish ethical world in which Jesus, James, and other New Testament voices taught.

Reading guardrails

Claims this page does not make

Sirach is not Ecclesiastes.

Ecclesiasticus is a Latin title for Sirach; Ecclesiastes is a different book associated with Qoheleth.

The Greek translation is not the original composition.

Ben Sira wrote in Hebrew; his grandson later translated the work into Greek.

New Testament parallels are not automatically quotations.

Recent scholarship distinguishes close echoes and shared wisdom traditions from formal citation.

Ancient social assumptions are not self-interpreting commands.

Statements about women, slaves, household order, status, and honor require historical context and whole-Scripture discernment.

Noncanonical in Judaism does not mean unused by Jews.

Hebrew preservation and later Jewish reception show substantial continued engagement with Ben Sira.

Canonical status differs by Christian tradition.

Catholic and Orthodox churches receive Sirach as Scripture; Protestant traditions do not include it in the Old Testament canon.
