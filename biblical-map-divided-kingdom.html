Biblical Maps • 1 Kings 12–2 Kings

The Divided Kingdom

After Solomon’s death, the monarchy divided into the northern kingdom of Israel and the southern kingdom of Judah. This map emphasizes that the border was not fixed for two centuries: capitals changed, border towns changed hands, neighboring kingdoms regained independence, and imperial pressure repeatedly reshaped the political landscape.

Accuracy note: The overlays are broad period-based teaching regions, not permanent surveyed borders. Bible Mapper notes that the borders of Israel, Judah, Moab, Edom, Aram, and neighboring states shifted repeatedly. A border that fits one king’s reign can be misleading for another.
The north had more than one capital. Jeroboam first fortified Shechem and Penuel. Tirzah later served as the northern royal capital, and Omri eventually established Samaria as the enduring capital. Jerusalem remained the Davidic capital of Judah. The map lets you toggle royal centers and approximate kingdom cores rather than collapsing the whole period into one moment.

Map key

Read the kingdoms by period, not as modern states.

Northern kingdom: Israel

Broad core territory associated with Israel after the split, with Samaria as the later capital.

Southern kingdom: Judah

Broad core territory centered on Jerusalem and the Judean hill country.

Fluid / contested zone

Border country where control changed, especially around Benjamin, Ramah, Geba, Mizpah, and northern approaches to Judah.

Royal / strategic site

Capital, fortified city, or major regional center.

Jeroboam’s cult centers

Dan and Bethel are shown separately because of their role in 1 Kings 12.

Places to know

The political geography behind Kings and Chronicles.

Shechem

Rehoboam went to Shechem for the coronation gathering where the rupture became public. Jeroboam then fortified Shechem as an early northern center.

1 Kings 12:1,25

Tirzah

Tirzah served as the royal seat for several northern kings before Omri established Samaria.

1 Kings 14:17; 15:21,33; 16:6–23

Samaria

Omri purchased the hill of Samaria and built the capital that remained the center of the northern kingdom until Assyria captured it.

1 Kings 16:24; 2 Kings 17

Jerusalem

The southern kingdom retained Jerusalem as the Davidic capital and Temple city.

1 Kings 12:17–21

Dan & Bethel

Jeroboam established royal cult centers at the northern and southern ends of his kingdom to discourage pilgrimage to Jerusalem.

1 Kings 12:26–33

Ramah / Geba / Mizpah

These towns illustrate why the Israel–Judah border must be treated as fluid. Baasha fortified Ramah; Asa dismantled it and strengthened Geba and Mizpah.

1 Kings 15:16–22; 2 Chronicles 16:1–6

Megiddo & Jezreel

These northern strategic locations help orient the Jezreel Valley and the military geography of Israel’s kings.

1 Kings 9:15; 21; 2 Kings 9

Lachish

A major fortified city guarding Judah’s western approaches, especially important during the later Assyrian period.

2 Kings 18:14,17; 19:8

Reference approach

One map, multiple moments.

The period model is cross-checked with Bible Mapper’s “The Kingdoms of Israel and Judah,” “Poster Map of Israel and Judah,” and “Border Conflict between Israel and Judah.” Key modern identifications are cross-checked with OpenBible.info: Samaria at Sebaste/Sebastiyeh, Tirzah at Tell el-Farah, Bethel at Beitin, Dan at Tel Dan, Megiddo at Tel Megiddo, Jerusalem, and Lachish at Tell ed-Duweir. The polygons are intentionally broader than the site pins because political control changed across the period.
