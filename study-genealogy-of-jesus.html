Standalone Bible Study · Matthew 1:1–17 · Luke 3:23–38

Why Two Genealogies?

The family lists at the beginning of Matthew and in Luke are easy to skip. Slow down, and they become a doorway into the identity of Jesus, the faithfulness of God, the messiness of human history, and the way each Gospel tells the good news.

Open Genealogy Reference

Big Question

If Matthew and Luke give different genealogies, what are they trying to show us about Jesus?

Big Truth: Both Gospels root Jesus in real history. Matthew emphasizes Jesus as the promised Son of Abraham and royal Son of David; Luke carries Jesus’ line through David to Abraham and all the way to Adam. The differences should be studied honestly without forcing certainty where the Gospel writers do not explain every genealogical detail.

Open Your Bible

Read the passages before solving the puzzle.

  1. Matthew 1:1–17 — Notice the starting point, repeated names, women mentioned, the line through David, the exile, and Matthew’s three-part arrangement.
  2. Luke 3:21–38 — Start with Jesus’ baptism and then follow the genealogy backward. Notice where David appears, which son of David is named, and how far Luke continues.
  3. 2 Samuel 7:8–16 — Read the promise connected to David’s house and kingdom.
  4. Genesis 12:1–3 — Read the promise connected to Abraham and blessing for the nations.

Observation first: Before asking how the two lists fit together, ask what each Gospel writer actually chose to emphasize.

1 · Genealogies are not filler

Names can carry covenant, kingship, memory, and identity.

Modern readers often treat genealogies as the pages to skip. Biblical writers did not. Family lines could establish inheritance, tribal identity, priestly legitimacy, royal succession, covenant continuity, and connection to earlier promises.

Matthew opens his Gospel by naming Jesus in relation to David and Abraham. Before Jesus teaches a parable or performs a miracle, Matthew is already telling the reader where Jesus belongs in the story.

Luke places his genealogy after Jesus’ baptism and before the temptation. He traces Jesus beyond Israel’s patriarchs all the way to Adam. That placement and scope reinforce the Gospel’s widening horizon.

2 · Matthew’s emphasis

Promise, kingship, exile, and Messiah.

Matthew moves forward from Abraham. The line reaches David, then continues through Solomon and Judah’s royal line. Matthew also shapes the genealogy into a memorable three-part pattern connected to Abraham, David, the exile, and the Messiah.

That structure is theological and literary as well as genealogical. Matthew is not merely giving background information. He is presenting Jesus as the climax of Israel’s covenant story.

Notice the women

Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, “the wife of Uriah,” and Mary interrupt what could otherwise look like a polished male royal list. Their stories carry outsider status, courage, vulnerability, sexual scandal, injustice, grief, and grace. Matthew does not erase the broken parts of Israel’s story on the way to Jesus.

3 · Luke’s emphasis

Jesus belongs to Israel’s story and humanity’s story.

Luke moves backward from Jesus. After Joseph, the names differ from Matthew’s line. When Luke reaches David, he goes through Nathan rather than Solomon. Then he keeps going: Abraham, Noah, Adam, and finally God.

Luke’s scope does not make Israel unimportant. It places Israel’s story inside the wider human story. The Savior connected to David and Abraham is also connected to Adam.

This fits a Gospel that repeatedly shows the good news crossing boundaries and reaching people who may have assumed they were outside the center of the story.

4 · The difference is real

Faithful reading does not pretend the lists are identical.

After David, Matthew follows Solomon while Luke follows Nathan. Matthew names Jacob as Joseph’s father; Luke names Heli in the line connected to Joseph. These are not small spelling differences.

The right response is neither panic nor denial. The question is what kind of genealogies these are, how ancient genealogical conventions worked, and what explanations remain possible.

Major proposals Christians have considered

  • Royal/legal line and family line: Matthew may preserve the royal succession connected to David’s throne while Luke preserves another family line.
  • Joseph and Mary proposal: Some Christians understand Matthew as Joseph’s line and Luke as Mary’s line expressed through Joseph. This is possible, but Luke does not explicitly say that Heli is Mary’s father.
  • Levirate/legal fatherhood: Ancient family law could produce legal and biological relationships that do not map neatly onto a modern one-father-one-line chart.
  • Genealogical compression: Biblical genealogies can skip generations and use father/son language more broadly for ancestors and descendants.

The text gives enough information to see the theological purpose clearly, but not enough to prove every proposed reconstruction with certainty.

Context in Plain English

Matthew and Luke are not trying to answer every modern genealogy question.

A modern family-tree service usually aims at biological descent with exhaustive documentation. Ancient biblical genealogies can be more selective. A writer may organize a line around covenant, inheritance, dynasty, tribal identity, or a memorable literary structure.

Matthew’s deliberate arrangement shows selectivity openly. Luke’s much longer line serves a different narrative purpose. Reading them faithfully means respecting what the authors are doing rather than forcing both lists into the expectations of a twenty-first-century ancestry database.

Jesus Connection

The lists are not ultimately about the ancestors. They are about the One who arrives at the end of the story.

Matthew says: look at the promises, the kings, the exile, the failures, the outsiders, and the generations of waiting. Jesus has come.

Luke says: keep tracing backward. David. Abraham. Noah. Adam. Jesus enters not only Israel’s story but the human story.

The genealogy points toward incarnation. The eternal Son does not remain distant from human history. He enters a family line marked by dignity and disgrace, faithfulness and sin, power and vulnerability. God’s saving work comes into the real world.

Don’t Confuse These

Keep the study careful.

  • Different genealogies ≠ permission to pretend there is no difficulty. The difference is real and deserves honest study.
  • Different genealogies ≠ automatic proof of contradiction. Ancient legal, dynastic, and genealogical conventions leave more than one reasonable explanation.
  • “Luke is Mary’s genealogy” ≠ something Luke explicitly says. It is a historic proposal and should be labeled that way.
  • Flawed ancestors ≠ God approving their sins. Redemption does not sanitize evil.
  • Genealogical connection ≠ salvation by bloodline. The genealogies identify Jesus; the Gospel calls people to faith and discipleship.

Think It Through

What do the names teach before we explain every difference?

  1. Why might Matthew begin the whole Gospel with Abraham and David?
  2. What does Luke gain by carrying the genealogy all the way to Adam?
  3. Why do you think Matthew intentionally brings Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, Uriah’s wife, and Mary into view?
  4. What do the genealogy’s failures and scandals teach about God’s faithfulness?
  5. Why is it important to distinguish what a text states from an interpretation we use to explain it?
  6. How can a Christian face a difficult Bible question without hiding it or exaggerating the certainty of an answer?

Go Deeper

Trace four promises through the family line.

Read these passages and write one sentence beside each explaining how it adds to the genealogy’s meaning:

  • Genesis 3:15 — hope after humanity’s fall.
  • Genesis 12:1–3 — Abraham and blessing to the nations.
  • 2 Samuel 7:8–16 — David, kingdom, and royal promise.
  • Isaiah 11:1–10 — hope from Jesse’s line and a kingdom reaching the nations.

Then return to Matthew 1 and Luke 3. Ask: What kind of Jesus are these writers preparing us to meet?

Live It

God’s faithfulness is bigger than a spotless family story.

Many people carry complicated family histories. Some are grateful for their background. Some carry grief, estrangement, addiction, abuse, failure, secrecy, or patterns they are determined not to repeat.

The genealogy of Jesus does not teach that every family event was God’s will or that harm should be minimized. It does show that broken human history is not beyond God’s redemptive reach.

This week: thank God for one gift that came through people before you, name one unhealthy pattern that should stop with you, and choose one Christlike practice you want to pass to the generation after you.

Leader Note

Do not turn this into a debate contest.

The goal is biblical literacy and a clearer view of Jesus, not proving that one reconstruction of the genealogies is the only possible Christian answer.

Make the distinction between text and proposal explicit. If someone says, “Luke is definitely Mary’s genealogy,” acknowledge that this is a longstanding Christian explanation, then point out that Luke itself does not state that directly.

If family-history discussion surfaces adoption, infertility, estrangement, abuse, unknown parentage, or grief, do not force personal disclosure. Genealogy can be emotionally loaded. Let participants answer generally or pass.
