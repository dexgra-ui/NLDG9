Other Ancient Writings · Detailed Guide

1 and 2 Maccabees

Two independent Jewish histories of the Maccabean crisis that overlap in events but differ sharply in authorship, structure, scope, style, and theology.

Seleucid Judea · second century BCE Antiochus IV and the Maccabean revolt Temple rededication and Hanukkah Canonical in Catholic and Orthodox traditions
These are not volume 1 and volume 2 by one author.

1 Maccabees and 2 Maccabees are independent works. They overlap for part of the same crisis, but 2 Maccabees does not begin where 1 Maccabees ends and does not continue its story.

How NLDG is approaching these writings

This guide separates historical reconstruction from each book’s theological interpretation. It also names canonical differences accurately: Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox Christians receive 1 and 2 Maccabees as Scripture, while Protestant traditions do not include them in the Old Testament canon. Jewish tradition remembers the Maccabean revolt and Hanukkah without including these books in the Tanakh.

Start here

Two independent books, not a two-volume sequel

The titles can mislead modern readers. Both books are important sources for the same broad historical crisis, but they were written by different authors for different purposes.

Historical timeline

From Seleucid rule to the Hasmonean rise

198 BCE

Seleucid power replaces Ptolemaic control over Judea. Greek language and culture were already widespread, and Jewish responses to Hellenistic culture were diverse.

175 BCE

Antiochus IV Epiphanes takes the Seleucid throne. Political competition for the Jerusalem high priesthood intensifies during his reign.

169–168 BCE

Antiochus campaigns in Egypt. Jerusalem experiences political upheaval, violence, and plundering connected to the wider imperial struggle.

167 BCE

Seleucid decrees suppress distinctive Jewish practices, the Temple is desecrated, and pagan worship is imposed. This coercive phase is the immediate setting for the revolt.

167–166 BCE

The priest Mattathias resists at Modein and calls others to defend the covenant. After his death, his son Judas becomes the revolt’s best-known military leader.

164 BCE

Judas and his forces regain the Temple, cleanse it, rebuild its altar, and rededicate it on the twenty-fifth of Kislev. The annual Feast of Dedication, Hanukkah, grows from this event.

160 BCE

Judas dies in battle. 2 Maccabees has already ended after the defeat of Nicanor; 1 Maccabees continues the family story.

160s–140s BCE

Jonathan and then Simon lead the movement through shifting Seleucid alliances and growing Jewish political autonomy.

142/141 BCE onward

Simon’s leadership marks effective independence from Seleucid tribute, helping establish the Hasmonean state that would shape Judea before Roman domination.

The crisis under Antiochus IV

Hellenization was not one simple process

Greek language, education, civic institutions, trade, and culture had been influencing Judea long before Antiochus IV. Some Jewish elites voluntarily sought Greek civic status and customs. 2 Maccabees 4, for example, portrays the high priest Jason as establishing a gymnasium in Jerusalem before the later persecution.

The crisis became coercive when royal policy moved beyond cultural influence into suppression of Jewish religious practices. The books describe bans on Torah observance, circumcision, Sabbath keeping, and traditional sacrifice, along with pagan cult practices imposed at the Jerusalem Temple.

Historians continue to debate the precise motives, sequence, and scope of Antiochus’s policies. Political revolt, imperial control, conflicts within Judean leadership, and religious coercion all belong in the discussion.

Keep two realities together

Before the decrees
Some Jews adopted Hellenistic customs voluntarily and selectively.
During the persecution
Seleucid policy compelled abandonment of core Jewish practices and imposed cultic conformity.
Reading caution
“Forced Hellenization” is useful shorthand for the coercive phase, but it should not imply that every earlier encounter with Greek culture was forced.

1 Maccabees

A historical narrative of revolt, leadership, and Hasmonean consolidation

1 Maccabees was probably written around 100 BCE by an anonymous Jewish author and was originally composed in Hebrew. The Hebrew text has not survived as a complete manuscript; the book is preserved through an early Greek translation whose style retains many Hebrew features.

Its narrative runs from Antiochus IV’s accession in 175 BCE to the end of Simon’s leadership in the 130s BCE. After the crisis and Mattathias’s call to revolt, the book follows Judas Maccabeus, then Jonathan, then Simon. This gives it a much broader dynastic and political scope than 2 Maccabees.

The author strongly admires the family of Mattathias and presents their victories as deliverance for Israel. That makes 1 Maccabees indispensable historical evidence, but it also means readers should recognize the author’s pro-Hasmonean viewpoint rather than treating the narrative as ideologically neutral modern historiography.

1 Maccabees 1–2

Crisis and revolt

Antiochus IV, Temple desecration, persecution, Mattathias at Modein, and the beginning of armed resistance.

1 Maccabees 3–9

Judas Maccabeus

Military victories, the Temple rededication, diplomacy, renewed conflict, and Judas’s death.

1 Maccabees 9–12

Jonathan

Survival, diplomacy, high-priestly leadership, shifting Seleucid alliances, and Jonathan’s capture.

1 Maccabees 13–16

Simon

Political independence, consolidation, dynastic legitimacy, and transition toward John Hyrcanus.

Theological emphasis: 1 Maccabees is not “secular history.” Covenant faithfulness, the law, Israel’s identity, and divine help frame the story, even though its presentation is generally less overtly miraculous than 2 Maccabees.

2 Maccabees

An epitome of Jason of Cyrene with a different theological center

2 Maccabees is not a sequel to 1 Maccabees. Its anonymous editor explicitly says that the book is an epitome, or condensed retelling, of a five-volume history written by Jason of Cyrene. Jason’s larger work is lost.

The surviving book was composed in Greek and focuses on a narrower period. It begins before the persecution with the Jerusalem Temple and high-priestly conflicts, then narrates Antiochus IV’s assault, martyrdoms, Judas’s victories, Temple rededication, and later conflicts. It ends after the defeat of Nicanor, before the death of Judas and long before the later careers of Jonathan and Simon described in 1 Maccabees.

Its style is rhetorical and dramatic. The author interprets events through an explicit theological lens: sin brings chastisement, repentance brings mercy, martyrs expect resurrection, persecutors face judgment, and God intervenes for the Temple and the faithful.

What “epitome” means here

Earlier source
A lost five-volume history by Jason of Cyrene
Present author
An anonymous abridger or epitomist
Method
Selecting, arranging, and retelling material for a shorter work with its own rhetorical and theological purpose
Implication
The surviving book should not be treated as if every sentence came directly from Jason unchanged.

History and interpretation: Claims that Antiochus ruled, Jerusalem was attacked, the Temple was desecrated, persecution occurred, Judas led resistance, and the Temple was rededicated are historical claims that can be compared with other evidence. Explanations that these events were divine chastisement, miraculous deliverance, or judgment are the theological interpretation of 2 Maccabees and should be identified as such.

Martyrdom, resurrection & judgment

2 Maccabees makes suffering part of its theology of hope

2 Maccabees 6–7 contains the famous accounts of the elderly Eleazar and a mother with seven sons who refuse commands that would violate the law. Their deaths are presented not as meaningless defeat but as faithful witness before God.

The seven-brothers narrative gives unusually explicit expression to bodily resurrection. The martyrs expect the God who created life to restore life, while the persecutor is warned of divine judgment. This is one of the clearest Second Temple Jewish witnesses to resurrection theology before the New Testament era.

The book also interprets the martyrs’ suffering corporately, connecting their faithfulness with mercy toward the people. Later Jewish and Christian traditions developed these martyr stories in different ways.

Reading guardrail: The historical fact of persecution and execution should be distinguished from the book’s theological claims about the redemptive meaning of suffering, resurrection, and divine judgment.

A major interpretive difference

2 Maccabees 12:39–46 and prayer for the dead

After a battle, Judas’s men discover that some fallen Jewish soldiers had objects associated with prohibited idolatrous worship. The narrative describes a collection sent to Jerusalem for a sin offering and commends action on behalf of the dead in light of resurrection.

Roman Catholic interpretation: Because 2 Maccabees is canonical in Catholicism, this passage has been used as biblical support for prayer for the dead and for the conviction that the departed may benefit from the prayers of the faithful. Catholic theology connects this with its doctrine of purification after death.

Eastern Orthodox interpretation: Orthodox churches also receive 2 Maccabees as Scripture and practice prayer for the departed. Orthodox explanations of the intermediate state and postmortem purification, however, are not identical to later Roman Catholic scholastic formulations of purgatory.

Protestant interpretation: Protestant traditions do not include 2 Maccabees in the Old Testament canon, so the passage normally does not function as a canonical proof text for doctrine. Protestants may still study it as evidence for Jewish belief and practice in the Second Temple period.

What the disagreement is really about

Text
All traditions can study what 2 Maccabees 12 says.
Canon
Traditions disagree about whether the book has Old Testament canonical authority.
Doctrine
Traditions also differ in how they describe the state of the dead and the theological meaning of prayer for them.

Temple desecration & rededication

The historical background of Hanukkah

The crisis centered on the Jerusalem Temple as well as Jewish law. 1 Maccabees describes Antiochus’s forces plundering the sanctuary and later setting up a desecrating altar. 2 Maccabees gives its own theological and dramatic account of the Temple’s violation.

After Judas regained Jerusalem, the sanctuary was purified, a new altar was built, and worship was restored. 1 Maccabees 4 and 2 Maccabees 10 both describe an eight-day celebration beginning on the twenty-fifth of Kislev in 164 BCE and establish an annual observance of the dedication.

This is the historical foundation of Hanukkah, the Feast of Dedication. The famous miracle of one day’s oil lasting eight days appears in later rabbinic tradition, especially Babylonian Talmud Shabbat 21b; it is not the explanation given in 1 or 2 Maccabees.

From revolt to dynasty

The Hasmonean rise and the world before the New Testament

The family of Mattathias is commonly called the Hasmonean family. Judas’s victories began as resistance to persecution and Seleucid control, but the movement developed into a struggle for political autonomy. Jonathan and Simon combined military, diplomatic, political, and high-priestly leadership in ways that reshaped Judean government.

By the time of Simon, Judea had achieved substantial independence. The later Hasmonean dynasty expanded territory and joined kingship, priesthood, and political power in ways that produced both national strength and internal controversy.

This history matters for New Testament background. The memory of foreign domination, Temple desecration, armed resistance, priestly politics, national independence, and later Roman intervention all shaped the Jewish world into which Jesus was born. The Maccabean story helps explain that world; it should not be turned into a simple one-to-one political template for the first century.

Canon and reception

Different communities receive the books differently

Roman Catholic tradition

1 and 2 Maccabees are deuterocanonical books of the Catholic Old Testament and are received as inspired, canonical Scripture.

Eastern Orthodox traditions

1 and 2 Maccabees belong to the longer Old Testament received in Eastern Orthodox Christianity. Some Orthodox biblical traditions also include additional Maccabean books beyond these two.

Protestant traditions

1 and 2 Maccabees are not part of the Protestant Old Testament canon. Historically, many Protestant Bibles printed them in an Apocrypha section for reading and historical instruction; modern Protestant practice varies widely.

Jewish reception

The books are not part of the Tanakh. The revolt, Temple rededication, and Hanukkah remain central to Jewish historical memory, while later rabbinic tradition preserved and interpreted the festival through sources other than these books.

Language matters: Calling the books simply “noncanonical” is inaccurate without naming the tradition. They are canonical in Catholic and Orthodox Christianity, outside the Protestant Old Testament canon, and outside the Jewish Tanakh.

Biblical connections

Where Maccabean history helps readers understand canonical Scripture

Historical setting & interpretation

Daniel, Antiochus IV, and the desecration of the Temple

Daniel 8 and 11 contain imagery and historical detail widely connected in historical-critical scholarship with Antiochus IV, persecution, and the desecration of the sanctuary. Maccabean history supplies essential historical context for those chapters.

Historical correspondence does not by itself settle every claim about prophecy fulfillment. Jewish and Christian interpreters differ over whether Antiochus exhausts the prophetic horizon or also functions within broader typological or future-oriented readings.

Direct historical continuity

John 10:22 and the Feast of Dedication

John says that Jesus was in Jerusalem during the Feast of Dedication in winter. That feast is Hanukkah, whose historical origin lies in the Temple rededication described in 1 Maccabees 4 and 2 Maccabees 10.

John is not quoting Maccabees here; the connection is the shared historical festival.

Possible tradition echo

Hebrews 11:35 and Maccabean martyr traditions

Hebrews 11:35 remembers people who were tortured and refused release in hope of a better resurrection. Many interpreters see a possible relationship to the martyr stories of 2 Maccabees 6–7, where faithful Jews accept death while confessing resurrection hope.

The resemblance is strong enough to discuss, but uncertain literary dependence should not be presented as a direct quotation. Hebrews may be drawing on a wider Jewish martyr tradition known through or alongside 2 Maccabees.

Second Temple background

Why the revolt matters for the New Testament era

Maccabean and Hasmonean history stands between the Persian/Hellenistic world of later Old Testament history and the Roman world of the New Testament. It helps explain later debates about the Temple, priestly authority, foreign rule, Jewish identity, and hopes for deliverance.

Key themes

What to watch while reading

Faithfulness under pressure

Both books ask what covenant loyalty looks like when political power demands religious compromise.

Temple and worship

The sanctuary is a theological and political center of the crisis, especially in 2 Maccabees.

Resistance and leadership

1 Maccabees traces how a local revolt becomes an organized military and political movement under one family.

Martyrdom and resurrection

2 Maccabees places faithful death, future resurrection, and divine judgment near the center of its theological message.

Providence and interpretation

Both books interpret history through faith, but 2 Maccabees states divine causation and miraculous intervention much more explicitly.

Memory and identity

Hanukkah turns a historical deliverance and Temple restoration into recurring communal memory.

Reading guardrails

Claims this page does not make

1 Maccabees and 2 Maccabees are not consecutive volumes by one author.

They are independent works with different authors, structures, ranges of history, and theological emphases.

2 Maccabees does not continue where 1 Maccabees ends.

It overlaps part of the earlier period and ends while Judas is still alive.

Historical events and theological explanations are not identical claims.

The persecution and revolt can be historically investigated; divine chastisement, miraculous intervention, resurrection, and judgment are theological interpretations within the texts.

Hellenization was not uniformly forced from the beginning.

Some Jews adopted Greek culture voluntarily; the later Seleucid decrees created a distinct phase of coercive religious suppression.

Daniel’s Antiochus background does not settle every prophecy debate.

Historical correspondence should be distinguished from claims about complete or ultimate prophetic fulfillment.

Hebrews 11:35 is not labeled a direct quotation from 2 Maccabees.

A relationship to Maccabean martyr traditions is plausible and often proposed, but exact literary dependence remains uncertain.

Canonical status must be tradition-specific.

Greek Maccabees is not Ethiopian Meqabyan.
