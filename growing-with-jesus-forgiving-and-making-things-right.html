Forgiving and Making Things Right.

Forgiveness is not pretending nothing happened. God teaches mercy, truth, repentance, repair, wise boundaries, and patience while trust is rebuilt.

📖 Matthew 18:21–35Luke 19:1–10Colossians 3:12–13Romans 12:1820–30 minutesAges 7–11

Big Question

What does forgiveness mean, and what should happen when someone has been hurt?

Big Truth

God teaches us to forgive because He is merciful and forgiving toward us.

Forgiveness means releasing revenge and placing justice in God’s hands. It does not mean pretending wrong is okay, removing every consequence, trusting someone instantly, or staying in an unsafe situation. When we cause harm, repentance should include truth, apology, repair, and changed behavior.

Open Your Bible

Read and notice.

  1. Matthew 18:21–35 — Notice how Jesus connects the mercy we receive from God with the mercy we show others.
  2. Luke 19:1–10 — Pay attention to what Zacchaeus does after encountering Jesus.
  3. Romans 12:18 — Notice the words “if it is possible” and “as far as it depends on you.”

1

Forgiveness begins with God’s mercy.

Jesus’ story in Matthew 18 shows a servant who receives enormous mercy but refuses to show mercy to someone else.

The point is not that wrongdoing does not matter. The point is that people who have received God’s forgiveness should not live by revenge.

Christians forgive because God has been merciful to us.

2

Forgiveness is not pretending nothing happened.

Sometimes people say, “Just forgive and forget,” as if a hurtful event should disappear immediately. But forgiveness is not pretending.

If someone lies, the lie still needs to be addressed. If someone steals, what was taken may need to be returned. If someone bullies another child, adults may need to intervene. If someone has been unsafe, protection matters.

Remember:

Forgiveness can happen while consequences and boundaries remain.

3

Trust and forgiveness are not identical.

You may choose not to seek revenge and still need time before trusting someone again. Trust grows through truthful, safe, consistent behavior.

If someone repeatedly hurts you and says, “You have to trust me because you forgave me,” that is not a healthy use of forgiveness.

Children should never be pressured to stay alone with someone they believe is unsafe just to prove they have forgiven.

4

When you are wrong, making things right matters.

Zacchaeus had taken money unfairly. When his heart changed, he did not only say, “Sorry.” He took action to repair what he had done.

A good apology can include:

  • saying clearly what you did,
  • not blaming the other person,
  • recognizing how your action affected them,
  • asking forgiveness without demanding it,
  • repairing what you can,
  • changing your behavior.
Example:

“I told other people something private that I should not have shared. That was wrong. I am sorry. I will tell them I should not have said it, and I will not repeat it.”

5

Reconciliation may take time.

Reconciliation means rebuilding a relationship. Forgiveness and reconciliation are connected, but reconciliation usually requires participation from both sides.

Romans 12 recognizes that peace is not always fully in our control. If another person refuses to tell the truth, continues harmful behavior, or is unsafe, you cannot force a healthy relationship by yourself.

A trusted adult can help children decide what wise boundaries look like.

Let’s Think About It

Truth and mercy work together.

You say something mean to a sibling and later feel sorry. Tell the truth about what you said, apologize without excuses, ask what would help repair the situation, and work on speaking differently next time.

A classmate has bullied you repeatedly and says, “You are a Christian, so you have to forgive me and stop telling the teacher.” Forgiveness does not require hiding ongoing harm. Telling a trusted adult is appropriate. The behavior still needs accountability and change.

Talk About It

Questions for a child and trusted adult.

  1. What does Jesus’ story in Matthew 18 teach about mercy?
  2. Why is forgiveness different from pretending nothing happened?
  3. Can someone be forgiven and still face consequences? Explain.
  4. What helps rebuild trust?
  5. What did Zacchaeus do that showed real change?
  6. What makes an apology more meaningful than simply saying “sorry” quickly?
  7. When might a trusted adult need to help with a conflict?

Try It This Week

Truth, sorry, repair, change.

If you realize you have hurt someone, use this pattern:

  • Truth — Name what you did without excuses.
  • Sorry — Give a sincere apology.
  • Repair — Ask what can reasonably be made right.
  • Change — Choose a different action next time.

If someone has hurt you, you may pray about forgiveness and talk with a trusted adult about wise next steps. You do not have to handle serious harm alone.

Memory Verse

Colossians 3:13

Learn the verse from the Bible translation your family, church, or class uses. Talk about how Christian forgiveness is connected to the Lord’s forgiveness.

Prayer

Ask God for mercy and wisdom.

Father, thank You for Your mercy and forgiveness. Help me not hold on to revenge. When I hurt someone, give me courage to tell the truth, apologize, repair what I can, and change. When someone hurts me, give me wisdom about forgiveness, trust, and healthy boundaries. Help the adults who care for me make wise and safe decisions. In Jesus’ name, amen.

Parent / Teacher Note

Teach forgiveness carefully and never use it to silence a child.

Main objective: Help children understand that forgiveness is not secrecy, instant trust, removal of consequences, or returning to unsafe relationships.

Do not require a child to face, hug, contact, reconcile with, or verbally forgive a person who has harmed them as part of a group exercise. Do not ask children to publicly name people who hurt them.

Where abuse, grooming, violence, sexual misconduct, threats, or serious bullying are involved, safety and proper reporting come first. Forgiveness language must never be used to silence disclosure or bypass accountability. Follow safeguarding procedures and applicable reporting requirements. Do not promise secrecy you cannot keep.

When discussing apologies, avoid forcing immediate forgiveness from the person who was hurt. The wrongdoer is responsible for repentance whether or not the relationship is restored quickly.

This study does not request or collect personal stories from children.

Suggested closing question: How can truth and forgiveness work together instead of against each other?
