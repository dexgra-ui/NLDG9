1 Enoch

A layered Second Temple Jewish collection about the Watchers, judgment, heavenly mysteries, sacred time, and the hope that God will finally set a disordered world right.

Keep two truths together.

Jude directly uses material from 1 Enoch, and the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church receives 1 Enoch as Scripture. Neither fact means every Christian tradition has historically treated the entire collection the same way.

How NLDG is approaching this writing

What is 1 Enoch?

1 Enoch is not one book written at one moment. It is a collection of Enochic writings composed and edited over time. Modern scholarship commonly treats its major sections as distinct works with their own literary histories, later transmitted together under the name of Enoch.

The biblical Enoch appears briefly in Genesis 5:21–24 as the seventh generation from Adam, a man who “walked with God” and was taken by God. Later Jewish writers developed Enoch into a visionary sage who receives heavenly knowledge and announces divine judgment.

The collection matters because it preserves an important stream of Jewish apocalyptic interpretation from the centuries surrounding the New Testament. It helps readers see how some Jews in this period thought about angels, evil, judgment, resurrection, cosmic order, sacred time, and the vindication of the righteous.

Structure

The major sections inside 1 Enoch

The familiar five-part outline is useful for study, but it should not be mistaken for evidence of a single author or single date.

1–36

Book of the Watchers

Expands the Genesis 6 tradition into a story of rebellious heavenly beings, forbidden knowledge, giants, judgment, and Enoch’s heavenly journeys.

Its compositional history reaches back into the third century BCE, with some traditions possibly earlier.
37–71

Book of Parables / Similitudes

Three visionary discourses centered on judgment, the righteous, heavenly revelation, and an exalted figure called the Chosen One or Son of Man.

Its date remains debated. Many scholars place it near the turn of the era. No identified Qumran fragments preserve this section.
72–82

Astronomical Book / Book of Luminaries

Describes heavenly cycles, calendrical order, the sun, moon, stars, and the conviction that creation follows an ordered pattern revealed by God.

It belongs to the earliest Enochic material and is represented among the Aramaic manuscripts from Qumran.
83–90

Dream Visions

Includes a flood vision and the Animal Apocalypse, which retells large portions of biblical history through symbolic animals and apocalyptic imagery.

Often associated with Second Temple political crises; precise dating and internal literary development remain debated.
91–108

Epistle of Enoch and concluding materials

Contains exhortation, judgment teaching, the Apocalypse of Weeks, wisdom-like instruction, and later concluding traditions including material about Noah.

This final block contains multiple literary units rather than one simple composition.

Transmission

How the text survived

Ancient Jewish period

Enochic writings circulated in the Second Temple period. Aramaic fragments recovered among the Dead Sea Scrolls demonstrate that major portions of the collection existed before the Christian era.

Qumran evidence

Fragments from four of the five major Enochic sections have been identified at Qumran. The Parables / Similitudes are the notable exception.

Greek and other witnesses

Parts of 1 Enoch also survive in Greek and in smaller witnesses in other ancient languages, showing that the work circulated beyond a single linguistic community.

Ethiopic preservation

The complete received collection survives through the Geʽez manuscript tradition of Ethiopian Christianity. This preservation is essential to modern knowledge of 1 Enoch.

Important: The survival of a complete Ethiopic text does not mean every section was originally composed in Geʽez. The collection has a long transmission history across languages and communities.

Canon and reception

Who receives 1 Enoch as Scripture?

Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo

1 Enoch belongs to the biblical canon of the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, whose canon is broader than the Protestant, Roman Catholic, and Greek/Slavonic Eastern Orthodox collections.

Protestant traditions

1 Enoch is not part of the Protestant Old Testament canon, though it remains historically important for understanding Second Temple Judaism and the New Testament world.

Roman Catholic tradition

1 Enoch is not included in the Roman Catholic biblical canon. Catholic biblical scholarship nevertheless studies it as important ancient Jewish literature.

Greek / Slavonic Eastern Orthodox traditions

1 Enoch is not normally part of the standard Greek or Slavonic Orthodox biblical collections. Ethiopian Orthodox reception should therefore not be flattened into a generic claim about all Orthodox churches.

Avoid the shortcut: It is misleading to say simply that “the church removed Enoch from the Bible.” Ancient Christian communities did not all develop identical canon lists at the same time, and Ethiopian Christianity preserved a different canonical tradition.

Biblical connections

Where 1 Enoch intersects with canonical Scripture

These relationships are not all equal. The labels below distinguish direct quotation from interpretive expansion and broader literary or theological background.

Canonical starting point

Genesis 5:21–24

The biblical Enoch “walked with God” and was taken by God. 1 Enoch develops this brief figure into a heavenly traveler, scribe, mediator, and recipient of revelation.

Interpretive expansion

Genesis 6:1–4 and the Watchers

Genesis speaks of the “sons of God,” human women, and the Nephilim. 1 Enoch 6–16 expands this short passage into a detailed account of rebellious Watchers, giants, forbidden knowledge, and divine punishment.

That expansion is an ancient interpretation of Genesis, not wording supplied by Genesis itself.

Direct quotation

Jude 14–15 and 1 Enoch 1:9

This is the clearest connection. Jude attributes a prophecy to Enoch, and the passage corresponds to 1 Enoch 1:9. This is not merely a thematic similarity.

Possible literary background

Daniel 7, the Parables, and “Son of Man” language

The Parables of Enoch portray an exalted Son of Man / Chosen One in scenes of judgment. Scholars debate the dating of the Parables and their relationship to New Testament Son of Man language.

Themes to notice

What 1 Enoch helps us see about the biblical world

Cosmic rebellion and evil

The Watchers story locates part of the world’s corruption in angelic rebellion and forbidden teaching, offering an ancient Jewish explanation of why violence and disorder became so deep.

Judgment and restoration

Again and again, 1 Enoch insists that injustice is temporary, the wicked will answer to God, and the righteous will ultimately be vindicated.

Heavenly revelation

Enoch journeys through cosmic spaces and receives knowledge through angelic interpreters, a pattern important for understanding Jewish apocalypse as a literary form.

Ordered creation

The Astronomical Book presents heavenly time and movement as ordered knowledge, connecting worship, calendar, and creation.

Scripture being interpreted

1 Enoch demonstrates that ancient Jewish readers did not only preserve biblical texts; they also wrestled with brief, difficult passages such as Genesis 6 and developed substantial interpretive traditions around them.

Language later heard in Christianity

Judgment, resurrection, angels, demons, heavenly books, final vindication, and an exalted human-like judge all appear in the religious environment from which early Christianity emerged.

Reading guardrails

Claims this page does not make

Not one author at one date.

“1 Enoch” names a collection with multiple compositional layers.

Not the same as 2 Enoch or 3 Enoch.

Those are different works from different periods and linguistic traditions.

Not proof of a universal Christian canon.

Ethiopian canonical reception is real and should be respected, but it is not identical to Protestant, Catholic, or Greek/Slavonic Orthodox reception.

Not a license to import every detail into Genesis.

1 Enoch is an ancient interpretation of Genesis 6, not a missing chapter of Genesis.

Not evidence that every New Testament similarity is a quotation.

Only some relationships can be demonstrated directly. Others are shared traditions or debated parallels.

Not a “secret book” recovered only recently.

The work was known in antiquity and remained fully preserved in Ethiopian Christianity even when it was largely unavailable in Western Europe.
