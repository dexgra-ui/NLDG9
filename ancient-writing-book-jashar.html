Book of Jashar

A lost Israelite source explicitly named in Joshua 10:13 and 2 Samuel 1:18. Scripture preserves its title and material associated with it, but no independently identifiable ancient manuscript of the complete work survives.

Joshua 10:13 2 Samuel 1:18 Lost ancient source Likely poetic / heroic material
A title survives. The book does not.

Modern books called Jasher or Sefer haYashar should not be identified with the biblical source merely because they share its title.

How NLDG is approaching this lost source

This page separates what Scripture actually preserves from what later readers have tried to reconstruct. We can identify two clear canonical references, discuss the kind of material those references suggest, and trace later same-title works. We cannot produce the lost ancient book itself.

What does “Jashar” mean?

The Hebrew title is Sefer haYashar. “Jashar” or “Jasher” is a traditional English transliteration of the Hebrew word yashar, meaning “upright” or “right.” The title can therefore be rendered Book of Jashar, Book of the Upright, or Scroll of the Upright.

That matters because “Jashar” is not necessarily the name of an author. The title itself does not tell us who compiled the work, when it reached its final form, or exactly what “the upright” referred to.

First explicit reference

Joshua 10:13 and the battle at Gibeon

Joshua 10 describes Israel’s battle against the Amorite coalition. In the middle of the account, the text contains poetic lines calling on the sun to stand over Gibeon and the moon over the Valley of Aijalon, then says that this material is recorded in the Book of Jashar.

The USCCB note understands the reference as pointing to the preceding poetic passage and describes Jashar as a work that recounted the exploits of Israel’s early heroes in epic style.

The citation therefore gives us a valuable clue about genre: at least some material associated with Jashar was poetic or elevated heroic tradition.

Dating guardrail: Joshua’s reference does not prove that the final Book of Jashar already existed in Joshua’s historical lifetime. The citation belongs to the literary form of the biblical book as transmitted to us.

Second explicit reference

2 Samuel 1:18 and David’s lament

After Saul and Jonathan die, David composes a lament beginning, “How the warriors have fallen.” Before presenting the poem, 2 Samuel says David ordered that it be taught to the people of Judah and that it is recorded in the Book of Jashar.

This reference strengthens the case that Jashar contained poetic material connected to national memory, heroic figures, warfare, and lament.

Because the Joshua material concerns an earlier period while 2 Samuel associates the collection with Davidic material, some scholars have inferred that Jashar may have been a growing anthology rather than a book written all at once.

What survives?

Only references and embedded material, not the complete source

No manuscript has been securely identified as the ancient Book of Jashar cited by Joshua and 2 Samuel. We therefore cannot reconstruct its full length, organization, authorship, date, or complete contents.

The closest thing to recoverable “Jashar material” is the biblical material explicitly connected with it. Even here, scholars may disagree over the exact boundaries of what Joshua is quoting and how much of David’s lament should be thought of as preserved from the collection.

The responsible conclusion is modest: Scripture preserves evidence that such a source was known, and it preserves material associated with that source, but the source as a separate book is lost.

A textual question

Is there a possible third reference in Greek 1 Kings?

Some discussions point to the Greek textual tradition of 1 Kings 8 as a possible additional connection. In the Septuagint tradition, a poetic passage associated with Solomon can be followed by wording referring to a “book of song” or a similar source title.

Because the Hebrew and Greek textual traditions differ here, and because the title is not preserved in the same straightforward form as Joshua 10:13 and 2 Samuel 1:18, NLDG treats this as a possible textual connection, not as a third certain occurrence.

Guardrail: The two secure references remain Joshua 10:13 and 2 Samuel 1:18.

Source use inside Scripture

What does it mean that the Bible cites a lost book?

Biblical writers could draw on songs, archives, chronicles, genealogies, oral traditions, and earlier written collections. Jashar is one of the clearest places where the canonical text explicitly names such a source.

That does not create a problem for biblical inspiration. Inspiration concerns the canonical text in the form received as Scripture; it does not require every source consulted or quoted by a biblical writer to be independently inspired or canonized.

The same basic distinction appears elsewhere when Scripture names the Book of the Wars of the Lord, royal annals, prophetic records, or when New Testament writers use material known from wider Jewish and Greco-Roman traditions.

The biggest identity trap

The later Sefer haYashar is not the lost biblical source

A Hebrew narrative also called Sefer haYashar became widely known in the early modern period. Its first securely documented printing is Venice, 1625. The work retells biblical history and incorporates material from Scripture, rabbinic literature, midrash, Josippon, and other traditions.

Its own preface claims an ancient transmission history, but that claim is not independently established. Modern scholarship has proposed dates ranging from the medieval period to the early sixteenth century; Joseph Dan argued on linguistic and contextual grounds for composition in Naples in the early sixteenth century.

This later Sefer haYashar can be valuable for studying Jewish legend and reception history. It should not be marketed or cited as though it were the actual ancient source named in Joshua and 2 Samuel.

A second identity trap

Jacob Ilive’s 1751 “Book of Jasher”

In 1751 the English printer Jacob Ilive published another work titled The Book of Jasher, presenting it as an ancient Hebrew text translated by the medieval scholar Alcuin.

Library catalogues identify Ilive as the author, and later editions describe the supposed Alcuin translation as fictitious. This work is separate from both the ancient lost Jashar and the Hebrew midrashic Sefer haYashar.

Because scans and reprints remain easily available online, readers can encounter it without realizing they are looking at an eighteenth-century production rather than an ancient Israelite source.

Do not confuse these works

Other Sefer haYashar titles

Title reuse

“Sefer haYashar” has also been used for unrelated Jewish ethical and legal works. A matching title alone never proves textual identity.

Canon and reception

Cited by Scripture does not mean separately canonical

Jewish canon

The lost Book of Jashar is named inside the Tanakh but is not itself preserved as a separate book of the Tanakh.

Roman Catholic tradition

The lost Jashar is not a separate book of the Catholic Old Testament canon. Joshua and 2 Samuel are canonical and preserve the references.

Eastern Orthodox traditions

The ancient Book of Jashar is not generally preserved as a separate canonical book in Orthodox Old Testament collections.

Protestant traditions

It is not part of the Protestant Old Testament canon. Its existence is known because the canonical books of Joshua and 2 Samuel name it.

Lost-source status

NLDG will not publish a modern “Book of Jashar” as though it were the lost biblical source.

The ancient work is not extant as an independently identifiable manuscript. NLDG can present the canonical passages that cite it, explain scholarly reconstruction, and separately study later books using the same title. Any future hosted later text must be labeled by its actual date, provenance, edition, and copyright status.
