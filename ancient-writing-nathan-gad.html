Records of Nathan and Gad

Chronicles closes David’s reign by pointing readers to records associated with Samuel the seer, Nathan the prophet, and Gad the seer. Nathan appears again among sources for Solomon. Those notices show a wider prophetic record-keeping world, but the original records are not independently recoverable today.

1 Chronicles 29:29–30 2 Chronicles 9:29 Prophetic historical records Lost / unverified source identity
Chronicles names three records in David’s source notice.

Samuel belongs in the verse alongside Nathan and Gad. The title of this guide follows the site’s existing resource label, but the evidence must keep all three prophetic witnesses visible.

How NLDG is approaching these records

This guide distinguishes the source notices in Chronicles from the canonical books of Samuel, from later pseudepigraphal writings, and from assumptions about who wrote which biblical book. The records may overlap with material now preserved in Scripture, but their exact literary identity cannot be demonstrated.

What does 1 Chronicles 29:29 actually say?

At the end of David’s reign, Chronicles says that the king’s deeds “from first to last” were written in the records or words of Samuel the seer, Nathan the prophet, and Gad the seer. The following verse broadens the scope to David’s reign, power, and the times that passed over Israel and surrounding kingdoms.

English translations vary between “records,” “chronicles,” “history,” “acts,” “events,” or “book.” The underlying Hebrew uses divrei, a flexible word that can refer to words, matters, events, or accounts.

That means we should be cautious about turning each phrase into a formal ancient title with a known cover page. Chronicles clearly points to source material associated with these prophetic figures; the exact physical form of those records is not preserved.

The three names

Samuel, Nathan, and Gad belong to different parts of David’s story

Samuel the seer

David’s early rise

Samuel anoints David and belongs to the earlier phase of David’s story. Samuel’s death is narrated before many later events of David’s reign.

Nathan the prophet

Royal covenant, correction, succession

Nathan delivers the promise concerning David’s house, confronts David after the Bathsheba/Uriah crisis, and later helps secure Solomon’s succession.

Gad the seer

Wilderness counsel and royal judgment

Gad advises David during his flight from Saul and later confronts him after the census, directing him toward the threshing floor that becomes associated with the future Temple site.

Guardrail: These biblical roles help explain why Chronicles associates the three men with David’s reign. They do not prove that every canonical episode came directly from one of three now-lost books.

Nathan’s record

Nathan is cited for both David and Solomon

1 Chronicles 29:29 includes Nathan among the records covering David’s reign. Then 2 Chronicles 9:29 points to the records of Nathan the prophet among sources for Solomon, alongside the prophecy of Ahijah the Shilonite and the visions of Iddo the seer.

This gives Nathan a wider source role than a single episode in David’s life. His biblical ministry itself bridges David’s mature reign and Solomon’s accession.

What we cannot establish is whether 1 Chronicles 29:29 and 2 Chronicles 9:29 refer to one continuous Nathan collection, multiple records associated with Nathan, or source material known under flexible descriptive labels.

Gad’s record

Gad appears across David’s career

Gad first appears during David’s years of flight, telling him to leave the stronghold and return to Judah. Later, after David’s census, Gad is repeatedly called David’s seer and delivers God’s word of judgment and instruction.

In 1 Chronicles 21, Gad directs David to build an altar on the threshing floor of Ornan. That episode becomes especially important in Chronicles because the location is tied to the future Temple.

2 Chronicles 29:25 also remembers Gad and Nathan alongside David in connection with arrangements for Levitical music in Temple worship. Chronicles therefore remembers both prophets not only as speakers to the king, but as figures connected with enduring royal and worship traditions.

Relationship to canonical Samuel

Are these records simply our books of Samuel?

Jewish and Christian interpreters have long proposed that material associated with Samuel, Nathan, and Gad contributed to the canonical history now preserved in 1–2 Samuel. The proposal is understandable: Samuel dies before the end of the story, while Nathan and Gad remain active later in David’s life.

But Chronicles does not explicitly say, “these three men wrote the canonical books now called Samuel.” Nor do we possess separate ancient copies of the cited records that can be compared line by line with 1–2 Samuel.

The safest conclusion is that Chronicles knows or invokes prophetic records associated with these figures. Those records may stand behind some canonical material, but the exact editorial path is not recoverable.

A later manuscript with the same attribution

What about the extant Words of Gad the Seer?

A Hebrew pseudepigraphal work called Words of Gad the Seer survives in a manuscript associated with the Jewish community of Cochin, India, now held at Cambridge. The surviving copy is late, and Meir Bar-Ilan published a modern scholarly edition after extended study of the manuscript.

The composition date is debated. Scholarly proposals have ranged from antiquity to the medieval or early modern period. The text contains biblical-style Hebrew, apocalyptic material, expansions around David, and unusual scribal features.

Because the manuscript is much later than David’s period and its underlying composition date is disputed, it must not simply be declared the same document that 1 Chronicles 29:29 cites.

Identity guardrail: “Same attributed prophet” is not the same thing as “same ancient source.” The extant pseudepigraphon is important evidence for later Jewish reception of Gad, not automatic recovery of Chronicles’ lost record.

Nathan and later claims

No securely identified ancient “Book of Nathan” survives independently

Chronicles names Nathan’s records in connection with both David and Solomon, but no independently preserved ancient manuscript can be securely matched to those notices.

Modern internet publications or alleged fragments attributed to Nathan require the same verification standard NLDG uses elsewhere: traceable manuscript provenance, scholarly description, textual history, and clear dating. A title alone is not enough.

Ancient historiography

Chronicles openly places its history inside a wider source world

The Chronicler repeatedly points readers toward prophetic records, royal annals, visions, genealogical sources, and other historical materials. Nathan and Gad are part of that larger pattern.

This does not mean every source was a “missing Bible book.” Ancient historians could cite or draw on records without those records themselves becoming part of the final canonical collection.

Theologically, the existence of sources does not compete with inspiration. It tells us something about the historical and literary means through which biblical history was preserved and shaped.

Canon and source use

Prophetic records are not automatically separate canonical books

Jewish canon

Chronicles is part of the Tanakh and names these records, but the records of Samuel, Nathan, and Gad are not preserved as separate books of the Tanakh under those source labels.

Roman Catholic tradition

The cited records are not separate books of the Catholic Old Testament. The canonical historical books preserve the source notices.

Eastern Orthodox traditions

The Chronicles source records are not generally separate canonical books in major Orthodox biblical collections.

Protestant traditions

The cited records are not separate Protestant Old Testament books. They are known because canonical Chronicles names them.

Lost-source status

NLDG will not reconstruct “Nathan” or “Gad” records by stitching together biblical passages.

The canonical narratives can show us where Nathan and Gad appear, and the later Words of Gad the Seer can be studied separately as a pseudepigraphal work. Neither approach produces a verified copy of the specific records Chronicles cites.
