Ethiopian Meqabyan

Three books preserved in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo biblical tradition, best approached with two habits at once: respect the tradition that receives them as Scripture, and be unusually careful about claims the surviving scholarship cannot yet verify.

Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo canon Ge'ez textual tradition Three Meqabyan books Not Greek 1 and 2 Maccabees
Start with the name problem.

English sources sometimes call these books “Ethiopian Maccabees.” That label easily creates a false identification. Meqabyan is not an Ethiopian translation of the Greek Maccabean histories about Antiochus IV, Judas Maccabeus, and Hanukkah.

How NLDG is approaching these writings

Meqabyan is a place where confidence must match the evidence. Its Ethiopian canonical reception and its difference from Greek Maccabees are well documented. Its exact composition dates, authorship, literary growth, and modern English textual history are much less secure. This page will not fill those gaps with confident guesses.

Start here

Meqabyan is not Greek Maccabees

The Ethiopian Meqabyan books are different works from the Greek books called 1 and 2 Maccabees. Roger Cowley’s study of the Ethiopian canon explicitly notes that the three Mäqabeyan books are not the same as the Greek Maccabean books or Pseudo-Josephus. Earlier Ethiopic-literature scholarship made the same distinction.

The familiar Greek Maccabean histories center on Seleucid Judea, Antiochus IV Epiphanes, Mattathias, Judas Maccabeus, the Temple rededication, and the historical background of Hanukkah. Those are not the defining narrative of Meqabyan.

The similarity of the English names is therefore a cataloging and translation trap. A modern publication can look “Ethiopian” while actually reproducing the familiar Greek Maccabees under a different label.

Evidence first

What can be said with confidence?

Canonical reception is clear

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church lists Meqabyan among its Old Testament books. Modern official church resources identify first, second, and third Meqabyan within the Ethiopic biblical collection.

The Ge'ez textual tradition is real

Catalogued Ethiopian manuscripts preserve the books in Ge'ez. British Library Or. 505 and Or. 506 are eighteenth-century witnesses to the Meqabyan textual tradition.

They are not the Greek Maccabean books

This distinction appears in specialist scholarship on the Ethiopian canon and Ethiopic Christian literature, not merely in modern popular summaries.

Dating is not secure

NLDG is not assigning a confident century of composition. A manuscript’s copying date tells us when that copy existed, not when the underlying work was first composed.

Authorship is not secure

No named historical author for each Meqabyan book is established in the accessible sources used for this guide. Traditional introductions and modern scholarship should be distinguished from demonstrable authorship.

English access is uneven

Modern English versions exist, but provenance, manuscript base, editorial method, and copyright status are not consistently documented. That makes source verification essential.

Canon and naming

Why Ethiopian book counts can look confusing

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church describes an 81-book biblical canon, but historical and modern lists do not always count individual units in exactly the same way. This is one reason a reader can see different totals or labels while looking at resources that are all trying to describe the Ethiopian tradition.

Cowley noted that the three Mäqabeyan books could be counted as two in one canon-law commentary because the second and third were reckoned together. The church’s English canon page likewise groups “II and III Maccabees” in one numbered line while its Amharic deuterocanonical directory distinguishes first, second, and third Meqabyan.

Guardrail: A difference in counting does not automatically mean a book has disappeared. Canon lists can count combined works differently from modern tables of contents.

Text & manuscripts

What the surviving evidence actually gives us

1906 · Josef Horovitz

Horovitz published an early specialist study, Das äthiopische Maccabäerbuch, placing the Ethiopic material into modern scholarly discussion. It remains important because later English-language scholarship on Meqabyan is comparatively sparse.

1926 · J. M. Harden

Harden’s survey of Ethiopic Christian literature emphasized that the Ethiopic book bearing the Maccabean name was not the familiar Greek Maccabean history. He described martyrdom, immortality and resurrection, and biblical-history material among its concerns.

1974 · Roger Cowley

Cowley documented the Ethiopian canon and the andemta commentary tradition, explicitly distinguishing the three Mäqabeyan books from the Greek Maccabean books and noting variable counting of the second and third books.

1721–1730 · British Library Or. 505

The British Library catalog records a Ge'ez Ethiopian manuscript under the title “Book of Maccabees.” The English catalog title should not be mistaken for proof that the text is Greek Maccabees.

18th century · British Library Or. 506

Another Ge'ez manuscript contains Meqabyan material alongside Ezekiel and other texts, providing a concrete manuscript witness to the Ethiopian tradition.

Today

The Ethiopian Orthodox Church continues to identify Meqabyan within its biblical canon, while English-language critical editions and transparent translations remain far less accessible than those of 1 Enoch or Jubilees.

Dating caution: The eighteenth-century manuscripts are witnesses to preservation, not dates of composition. NLDG will not turn a manuscript date into an authorial date.

The three books

A working orientation, not a pretend critical edition

Modern summaries commonly distinguish the books by literary character. Because dependable English critical access is limited, NLDG treats this outline as orientation rather than a final scholarly structure.

1 Meqabyan

Faithfulness against idolatrous power

Available descriptions present the first book as the most narrative of the three, centering resistance to an idolatrous ruler, suffering for faithfulness, divine judgment, and the vindication of those who refuse false worship.

NLDG is not assigning a secure author or composition date.
2 Meqabyan

Narrative moving into exhortation

Descriptions of the second book combine narrative material with extended instruction, warnings about sin and idolatry, and reflection on judgment, resurrection, and obedience.

Traditional counting sometimes joins the second and third books as one unit.
3 Meqabyan

Instruction and theological meditation

The third is commonly described as the shortest and more homiletic or meditative in character, using biblical examples to urge faithfulness and to reflect on temptation, judgment, resurrection, and the life to come.

Exact chapter divisions and English wording remain part of NLDG’s pending text verification.

Meqabyan

Preserved in the Ge'ez Ethiopian tradition.

Key themes

Recurring theological concerns

Faithfulness under pressure

Meqabyan repeatedly presents fidelity to God as more important than safety, status, or obedience to idolatrous power.

Rejection of idolatry

False worship functions as both a spiritual and moral crisis, making loyalty to God central to the books’ exhortation.

Judgment

The books stress that rulers and ordinary people alike answer to God and that present power is not the final verdict.

Resurrection and life to come

Older scholarship already noted resurrection and immortality themes in the Ethiopic material. Modern descriptions continue to identify resurrection as a major concern.

Obedience and instruction

The literature moves beyond story into exhortation, using remembered examples to call readers toward faithful living.

Scripture inside an Ethiopian interpretive world

Meqabyan belongs within a living Ge'ez and Amharic commentary tradition, not merely a Western list of “extra books.”

Biblical connections

Use thematic parallels without inventing literary dependence

Unlike Jude’s direct quotation of 1 Enoch, NLDG is not claiming a demonstrated canonical-biblical quotation from Meqabyan. The useful connections here are theological and interpretive.

Canon and reception

Say whose canon you mean

Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo

Meqabyan belongs to the Old Testament received by the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. It should not be described as merely “interesting extra literature” when discussing that tradition’s Bible.

Roman Catholic tradition

Meqabyan is not part of the Roman Catholic Old Testament canon. Catholic Bibles instead contain the distinct Greek books 1 and 2 Maccabees.

Eastern Orthodox traditions outside Ethiopia

The familiar Maccabean books in Byzantine/Greek and related Orthodox canons are distinct from Ethiopian Meqabyan. Orthodox canonical collections are not identical across all Eastern and Oriental churches.

Protestant traditions

Meqabyan is not part of the Protestant Old Testament canon and has rarely appeared in historic Protestant Apocrypha collections.

Important: “The Orthodox Bible” is not one completely uniform table of contents. Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo reception has features that should be described on their own terms.

What remains uncertain

Unknown is better than invented

No confident composition date yet.

NLDG has not found sufficiently secure evidence to assign each Meqabyan book a narrow century of composition.

No confident named author yet.

Traditional attributions, if encountered, must be labeled as tradition unless independently supported.

No assumption that surviving manuscripts are originals.

An eighteenth-century copy may preserve a much older work; the copy date alone cannot tell us how much older.

No automatic trust in an English edition labeled “Ethiopian Bible.”

The title on a modern book is not a textual pedigree. Source manuscript, translator, editorial method, and rights status must be checked.

No Greek-Maccabees substitution.

A text about Antiochus IV, Judas Maccabeus, and the Temple rededication should not be presented as Meqabyan merely because a publisher changes the title.

No invented Bible quotation claims.

The canonical links on this page are thematic comparisons unless a demonstrable textual relationship is established.
