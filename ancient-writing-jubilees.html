Jubilees

A second-century BCE Jewish retelling of Genesis and early Exodus that presents creation, covenant, law, festivals, and sacred time as parts of one ordered revelation.

Second century BCE Originally composed in Hebrew 364-day schematic calendar Canonical in Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Christianity
Read it as interpretation.

Jubilees retells Scripture rather than simply repeating it. It expands, rearranges, and explains Genesis and Exodus in ways that reveal how one ancient Jewish community understood covenant, law, angels, history, and holy time.

How NLDG is approaching this writing

This page is for historical, biblical, and theological study. Jubilees is received as Scripture in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, while other Christian traditions receive it differently. NLDG is not placing it inside the regular Old Testament or New Testament book sequence, and we distinguish the biblical text itself from Jubilees’ later interpretations and expansions.

What is the Book of Jubilees?

Jubilees is a Jewish rewriting of the biblical story from creation to Sinai. Oxford and Cambridge scholarship commonly place it in the second century BCE. A frequently cited scholarly range is approximately 163–140 BCE, though exact dating remains a matter of reconstruction rather than a date supplied by the book itself.

The work retells Genesis and material from the opening part of Exodus while presenting the story as revelation given to Moses at Sinai through an angel. Events are arranged within “jubilees,” cycles of forty-nine years made from seven periods of seven years.

Its Geʽez title, Mäṣḥäfä Kufale, means “Book of Division.” That title fits the book’s concern with dividing history, sacred time, people, land, and covenant life according to a divinely ordered pattern.

Narrative framework

Genesis and Exodus retold from Sinai

Jubilees presents familiar biblical history as though Moses is receiving its deeper ordering while on Mount Sinai.

Sinai frame

Moses receives heavenly revelation

The work opens at Sinai. An angel of the presence is commanded to recount history to Moses from heavenly tablets, giving the retelling a claim to revealed authority.

The framework is not found in Genesis itself; it is part of Jubilees’ interpretive presentation.
Creation & primeval history

Creation, Sabbath, Watchers, flood

Genesis 1–11 is expanded with stronger emphasis on Sabbath, angels, calendrical order, purity, the Watchers tradition, Noah, covenant, and the division of the earth.

Jubilees sometimes combines biblical material with traditions also known from 1 Enoch.
Patriarchal history

Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and covenant

The patriarchal stories are retold so that covenant practices, festivals, circumcision, separation from idolatry, and obedience appear deeply rooted before Sinai.

This is one of the book’s major interpretive strategies: later law is projected backward into ancestral history.
Joseph & Egypt

Israel’s family becomes a people

The Joseph cycle and the descent into Egypt are retold within Jubilees’ larger chronology, connecting family history with the covenantal identity of Israel.

The retelling remains recognizably biblical while adding chronology and interpretive detail.
Exodus & sacred time

Passover, deliverance, and law

The story reaches the exodus and Sinai with particular attention to Passover, Sabbath observance, festival timing, covenant obligations, and the proper ordering of the year.

Jubilees treats sacred time as part of the moral structure of creation, not merely as a later ritual calendar.

Manuscripts & transmission

How Jubilees survived

Hebrew origin

The discovery of Jubilees manuscripts among the Dead Sea Scrolls confirmed what earlier scholars had proposed: the work was composed in Hebrew.

Qumran evidence

A 2025 Oxford overview counts fourteen copies of Jubilees among the Dead Sea Scrolls, all in Hebrew. Older cataloguing sometimes counts fifteen fragmentary copies depending on how manuscript pieces are assigned.

Wide Second Temple use

Fragments were recovered from multiple Qumran caves, and Jubilees also influenced or interacted with other Second Temple Jewish writings. Its manuscript presence shows that it was more than an obscure later curiosity.

Geʽez preservation

The complete work survives in the Ethiopic tradition. Comparison with the Hebrew fragments from Qumran has shown that the Ethiopic text is an important and generally reliable witness to the earlier Hebrew work, though no translation tradition is mechanically perfect.

Other ancient witnesses

Portions or evidence of Jubilees also survive through Greek, Latin, and Syriac transmission, demonstrating that the work circulated across linguistic communities.

Why manuscript counts differ: Fragmentary Dead Sea Scroll material can be assigned differently by different catalogues. NLDG therefore avoids treating fourteen versus fifteen as a theological issue; the important point is that numerous Hebrew copies establish the work’s antiquity and Hebrew origin.

Sacred time

The 364-day calendar

Jubilees is deeply concerned with the correct ordering of time. Its year contains 364 days, or exactly fifty-two weeks. Because the year divides evenly into weeks, annual festivals remain attached to the same weekday within the system.

This calendar belongs to a wider Second Temple Jewish world in which different calendar systems competed. Related 364-day schemes appear in Enochic and Qumran literature.

It is common to call Jubilees’ system a “solar calendar,” and that shorthand is useful. Modern scholarship, however, cautions that the 364-day scheme is not simply the astronomical solar year. It is a schematic sacred calendar structured around complete weeks and liturgical order.

Canon and reception

Who receives Jubilees as Scripture?

Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo

Jubilees belongs to the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo biblical tradition. The Ethiopian canon is broader than Protestant, Roman Catholic, and Greek/Slavonic Eastern Orthodox collections.

Protestant traditions

Jubilees is not part of the Protestant Old Testament canon. It remains valuable for historical study of Second Temple Judaism and ancient biblical interpretation.

Roman Catholic tradition

Jubilees is not part of the Roman Catholic biblical canon. Catholic scholarship nevertheless studies it as important Jewish literature from the period before and around early Christianity.

Greek / Slavonic Eastern Orthodox traditions

Jubilees is not normally part of the standard Greek or Slavonic Orthodox biblical collections. Ethiopian Orthodox reception should not be generalized to every Orthodox church.

Important distinction: Describing Jubilees as “noncanonical” without naming a tradition is incomplete. It is noncanonical in many Christian traditions and canonical in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church.

Biblical connections

How Jubilees relates to canonical Scripture

Unlike the clear Jude quotation of 1 Enoch, Jubilees is most important for the way it rewrites and interprets biblical narratives rather than for an agreed direct quotation in the New Testament.

Rewritten Scripture

Genesis 1–11

Creation, Adam and Eve, Cain and Abel, Enoch, the Watchers, the flood, Noah, and the nations are retold with added chronology, angelic activity, covenant themes, and legal interpretation.

These additions show ancient interpretation; they should not be silently inserted into Genesis as though Genesis itself contains them.

Sinai reinterpretation

Exodus 19–20 and law before Sinai

While Exodus presents Sinai as the great covenantal law-giving moment, Jubilees repeatedly portrays patriarchs as already observing practices later associated with Torah. The effect is to present the law as rooted in creation and ancestral history rather than newly invented at Sinai.

Themes to notice

What Jubilees helps us see about the biblical world

Scripture being interpreted

Jubilees demonstrates that ancient Jewish readers actively explained difficult passages, harmonized traditions, filled narrative gaps, and applied Torah to changing circumstances.

One continuing covenant

The book strongly connects creation, Noah, Abraham, Israel, and Sinai within a unified covenantal story rather than treating them as isolated episodes.

Law before Sinai

Patriarchs are portrayed as keeping practices later codified in Torah. This gives the law primordial and ancestral authority.

Calendar as faithfulness

Correct sacred time is not a minor technical issue in Jubilees. Calendar, Sabbath, festivals, and covenant obedience belong together.

Angels and heavenly tablets

The work imagines earthly history as corresponding to heavenly revelation, with angels serving as witnesses and mediators of divine order.

Identity and separation

Jubilees emphasizes Israel’s distinct covenant identity in relation to surrounding peoples, practices, and calendars.

Reading guardrails

Claims this page does not make

Not a missing edition of Genesis.

Jubilees is an ancient reinterpretation and rewriting of biblical narratives, not an earlier “uncut” version of Genesis.

Not every expansion is historical fact.

Added names, angelic scenes, chronology, and legal details reveal interpretation unless independent evidence establishes more.

Not directly quoted by the New Testament in the way 1 Enoch is quoted in Jude.

Scholars discuss possible parallels, but NLDG will not label thematic similarity as direct literary dependence without stronger evidence.

Not “solar” without qualification.

The 364-day calendar is schematic and week-based; modern scholarship cautions against treating it as identical to the astronomical solar year.

Not proof of one universal Christian canon.

Ethiopian canonical reception is genuine, but Christian traditions have not all received Jubilees in the same way.

Not evidence that Torah was historically written in patriarchal times.

Jubilees presents patriarchs as Torah-observant for theological and interpretive reasons; that literary claim should not be confused with a modern historical conclusion.
