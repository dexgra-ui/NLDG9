Wisdom of Solomon

A Greek-speaking Jewish work that brings Israel’s wisdom tradition into conversation with Hellenistic philosophy while defending righteousness, divine justice, immortality, and Israel’s God.

Late first century BCE is a common scholarly setting Composed in Greek Likely Alexandrian Jewish context Canonical in Catholic and Orthodox traditions
Solomon is the literary voice, not the historical author.

The book speaks through the traditional figure of Solomon to give its teaching authority and continuity with Israel’s wisdom tradition. Modern scholarship does not treat the historical Solomon of 1 Kings as the actual author.

What is the Wisdom of Solomon?

Wisdom of Solomon is an anonymous Jewish work written in Greek. Its author knew Israel’s Scriptures in Greek and also knew Greek rhetoric and philosophical language. Alexandria in Egypt is the most common proposed setting because the book fits a Greek-speaking Jewish environment shaped by both Jewish Scripture and Hellenistic culture.

Oxford scholarship commonly places the work around the middle or later first century BCE, although proposed dates range more widely. The USCCB introduction similarly places it roughly fifty years before Christ and identifies the author as probably belonging to the Jewish community of Alexandria.

The book uses Solomon as a literary persona. This was a way of locating the teaching inside the established biblical memory of Solomon as Israel’s wise king, not a claim modern readers should confuse with demonstrable Solomonic authorship.

Structure

Three movements through righteousness, Wisdom, and Israel’s story

Scholars divide the book slightly differently at the seams, but its overall movement is clear.

Wisdom 1–5

Righteousness, persecution, and immortality

The book contrasts the reasoning of the ungodly with the destiny of the righteous. Apparent defeat and death do not have the last word because God’s justice reaches beyond what the wicked can presently see.

This section includes the famous portrayal of the righteous person in Wisdom 2 and the hope of the righteous in Wisdom 3.
Wisdom 6–9

Solomon praises and seeks Wisdom

Kings and rulers are called to learn justice, Wisdom is praised as a divine gift, and the Solomonic speaker prays for Wisdom rather than treating wisdom as mere human cleverness.

Wisdom 7–9 contains some of the book’s most influential language about personified Wisdom, creation, and divine radiance.
Wisdom 10–19

Wisdom and providence in Israel’s history

The final movement retells Israel’s past, especially the Exodus, through the activity of Wisdom and divine providence. Extended reflections on mercy, idolatry, creation, and judgment are woven into the retelling.

The biblical figures are often described without being named, inviting readers who know Israel’s Scriptures to recognize the stories.

Language & intellectual world

Jewish Scripture in conversation with Greek thought

Wisdom of Solomon is not simply a Greek philosophy book with Jewish names added. Its argument is deeply shaped by Israel’s Scriptures, especially wisdom traditions, creation theology, the Exodus story, and opposition to idolatry.

At the same time, the author uses Greek rhetorical techniques and philosophical vocabulary. Scholars have identified engagement with Platonic, Stoic, and other Hellenistic ideas, but the author adapts that language to a Jewish theological argument rather than surrendering Israel’s God to a Greek philosophical system.

This makes the book particularly useful for understanding how some Jews living in a Greek-speaking world could engage their surrounding culture while arguing for the truth and superiority of their inherited faith.

Personified Wisdom

Wisdom as gift, companion, and agent of God

Earlier biblical texts such as Proverbs 8 already personify Wisdom as a woman associated with God’s creative work. Wisdom of Solomon develops that tradition further. Wisdom is described with extraordinary language: she is intimately associated with God, participates in divine works, teaches virtue, and guides the righteous.

Wisdom 7:25–26 famously describes Wisdom with images of breath, emanation, reflection, and divine light. Those descriptions later became important in Christian theological reflection because some New Testament language about Christ sounds strikingly similar.

Guardrail: Similar theological vocabulary does not prove that a New Testament author copied Wisdom of Solomon directly. It does show that early Christian claims about Christ emerged in a Jewish world where highly developed language about divine Wisdom was already available.

Canon and reception

Who receives Wisdom of Solomon as Scripture?

Roman Catholic tradition

Wisdom of Solomon is part of the Catholic Old Testament and is commonly described as deuterocanonical. It appears within the biblical canon rather than as an appendix of merely historical works.

Eastern Orthodox traditions

Orthodox Christianity receives the Wisdom of Solomon as part of the longer Old Testament canon. It also appears in Orthodox liturgical readings.

Protestant traditions

Wisdom of Solomon is not part of the Protestant Old Testament canon. Historically, some Protestant Bibles printed it among the Apocrypha for reading and instruction without granting it the same canonical status as the Old and New Testaments.

Jewish tradition

The book is an ancient Jewish work, but it is not part of the Jewish biblical canon. Its Greek composition and later Christian transmission are important parts of its reception history.

Use precise language: Calling Wisdom simply “apocryphal” can hide major Christian differences. For Catholics and Orthodox Christians it belongs to Scripture; for Protestants it does not belong to the Old Testament canon.

Where Wisdom of Solomon intersects with canonical Scripture

Wisdom tradition

Probable Pauline echo

Wisdom 13–14 and Romans 1:18–32

Both texts reason from creation to knowledge of God and describe a descent from failure to honor the Creator into idolatry and moral disorder. Recent Oxford scholarship describes Paul as apparently echoing Wisdom of Solomon, but the New Testament does not formally quote the passage.

Possible literary echo

Wisdom 7:26 and Hebrews 1:3

Wisdom is described as a reflection of eternal light, while Hebrews describes the Son using language of divine glory and exact representation. The resemblance is important for the history of Jewish Wisdom language, but NLDG does not present it as a proven direct quotation.

Passion-narrative parallel

Wisdom 2 and the suffering righteous one

Wisdom 2 imagines the ungodly persecuting a righteous person who claims a special relationship with God and whose faithfulness becomes a test of whether God will vindicate him. Readers have long noticed resonances with Jesus’ passion, especially mockery that challenges God to deliver the righteous sufferer.

The parallel is meaningful, but it should not be turned into a claim that Wisdom 2 is a direct prediction of Jesus or that a Gospel writer explicitly cites it.

Themes to notice

What Wisdom of Solomon contributes to the biblical world

Righteousness beyond visible outcomes

The righteous may suffer and even appear defeated, yet God’s justice is not limited to present circumstances.

Immortality and hope

The book speaks more explicitly than much earlier biblical wisdom literature about the continuing destiny and vindication of the righteous after death.

Wisdom as divine gift

True wisdom is not merely intelligence. It comes from God, forms character, and teaches rulers to govern justly.

Idolatry as moral disorder

The critique of idols is not only about incorrect objects of worship. The author connects distorted worship with distorted desires and social life.

Creation reveals God

The created world bears witness to its Maker, making the turn toward created things as gods a failure of discernment.

Exodus as theological memory

Israel’s deliverance is retold as evidence of divine Wisdom, providence, mercy, judgment, and God’s ability to use creation itself in salvation and judgment.

Reading guardrails

Claims this page does not make

Not written by the historical Solomon.

Solomon functions as a literary persona that connects the book with Israel’s wisdom tradition.

Not a Hebrew book merely translated into Greek.

The work was composed in Greek and reflects sophisticated Greek rhetoric and vocabulary.

Not Greek philosophy replacing Jewish faith.

The author borrows and reshapes Hellenistic concepts inside a thoroughly Jewish theological argument.

Not directly quoted by the New Testament.

Recent scholarship recognizes echoes and knowledge of deuterocanonical literature while distinguishing those from formal quotation.

Not universally canonical or universally noncanonical.

Its status differs across Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, and Jewish traditions.

Not proof that every Wisdom-Christ parallel is literary borrowing.

Shared language may reflect a wider Jewish Wisdom tradition rather than direct dependence on this particular book.
