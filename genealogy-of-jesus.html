The Genealogy of Jesus.

Matthew and Luke both place Jesus inside a real human story, but they arrange and trace that story differently. This reference helps you see what each Gospel emphasizes, where the lines meet, where they diverge, and what we should not claim with more certainty than the text gives us.

At a glance

Two Gospel genealogies. One Jesus.

Genealogies in Scripture do more than preserve names. They establish identity, covenant connection, inheritance, royal claims, and continuity across generations. Matthew and Luke use genealogy as theology: both identify Jesus within Israel’s story, while Luke also stretches the line all the way to Adam.

Matthew 1:1–17

Abraham → David → Solomon → Joseph → Jesus

Matthew begins with Abraham and moves forward. The structure highlights Jesus as Son of Abraham and Son of David, placing special emphasis on covenant promise, Israel’s royal line, exile, and the arrival of the Messiah.

Luke 3:23–38

Jesus → Joseph → Nathan → David → Abraham → Adam

Luke begins with Jesus and moves backward. After David, Luke follows a different branch through Nathan rather than Solomon, and continues beyond Abraham to Adam, underscoring the worldwide human scope of Jesus’ mission.

Comparison chart

Where the lines agree, then separate.

This simplified chart shows the major anchors. It is intentionally not a modern DNA-style family tree. Biblical genealogies may be selective, may compress generations, and may serve legal, royal, covenant, or theological purposes as well as biological ones.

Matthew’s recorded line

Forward from Abraham · royal emphasis

Abraham→David→Solomon→Kings of Judah→Exile / Zerubbabel→Jacob→Joseph→Jesus

Luke’s recorded line

Backward from Jesus · universal emphasis

Jesus→Joseph→Heli→Nathan→David→Abraham→Noah→Adam
Important: Matthew explicitly calls Joseph the husband of Mary. Luke says Jesus was, “as was supposed,” the son of Joseph and then names Heli. The Gospels themselves do not explicitly say, “Matthew gives Joseph’s biological line and Luke gives Mary’s biological line.” That is one historic Christian proposal, but it should be presented as an interpretation rather than as a fact stated by Luke.

Why are they different?

Several explanations have been proposed.

ProposalWhat it suggestsWhat we can say carefully
Royal/legal line vs. family lineMatthew may emphasize dynastic succession through Solomon and Judah’s kings, while Luke preserves another family line through Nathan.This fits the different shapes of the lists, but the Gospels do not stop to explain the mechanics for us.
Joseph’s line vs. Mary’s lineA common Christian view says Matthew traces Joseph while Luke traces Mary’s ancestry through Joseph as the legal son-in-law of Heli.Possible, but Luke never explicitly says Heli is Mary’s father. Teach it as a proposal, not a certainty.
Levirate or legal fatherhoodAncient inheritance, remarriage, adoption, and levirate customs could allow a man to have one biological father and another legal/genealogical father.Early Christian writers used versions of this explanation. It shows that ancient genealogy categories are not always identical to modern family-tree expectations.
Genealogical compressionBiblical genealogies can intentionally skip generations and use “father/son” language for ancestors and descendants.Matthew’s carefully arranged structure makes selectivity especially visible. Omission of a generation does not necessarily mean error.

Matthew’s surprising names

Five women make the story impossible to reduce to a polished royal résumé.

Matthew’s genealogy names or deliberately references women whose stories include danger, outsider status, sexual scandal, grief, courage, and unexpected faithfulness. The point is not that every event in their stories was good. The point is that God’s redemptive work moves through real human history, not a sanitized one.

Reading guardrails

Don’t confuse these.

Difference ≠ automatic contradiction

The lists are plainly different after David, but ancient genealogies can serve different legal, dynastic, literary, and theological purposes. The difference should be examined rather than hidden.

Genealogy ≠ modern DNA report

Ancient writers did not share our expectation that every family tree must list every generation in a single biological sequence.

Messianic lineage ≠ moral perfection

The presence of deeply flawed people in Jesus’ ancestry is not an endorsement of their sins. It displays God’s faithfulness in a broken human story.

Ancestry ≠ salvation

Neither Gospel teaches that a person is reconciled to God by having the right family tree. The genealogies identify Jesus; the Gospel calls people to trust and follow Him.

Why it matters

The genealogy is already preaching Jesus.

Jesus is the promised King

Matthew places David at the center of the story. Jesus does not arrive disconnected from Israel’s hope; He comes as the Messiah within the covenant story.

Jesus enters the whole human story

Luke reaches beyond Abraham to Adam. Jesus belongs not only to one nation’s story but to humanity’s story.

Grace works through messy history

The family line contains saints and sinners, kings and outsiders, faith and failure. God’s faithfulness is not dependent on a spotless human record.

The incarnation is concrete

“The Word became flesh” means Jesus entered history, family, culture, vulnerability, and inheritance. Christianity is not built around an abstract idea but around the person of Jesus Christ.
